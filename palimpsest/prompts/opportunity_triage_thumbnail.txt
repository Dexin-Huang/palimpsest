# Opportunity triage (with thumbnail)

You are scoring a manuscript opportunity using both metadata and a low-resolution
thumbnail image. This is a second-pass triage for items that already looked
promising from metadata alone.

Your job is:
1. refine the existing triage judgment using visual evidence
2. avoid inventing exact content claims from the thumbnail
3. keep source-catalog facts, secondary evidence, and visual inference separate

## Non-Negotiable Rule

Do not assign an exact content identity from the thumbnail alone.

The thumbnail may:
- confirm unusuality
- weaken an earlier hypothesis
- reveal layout/script/diagram signals

The thumbnail may not justify a precise genre or title claim unless that claim
is already supported by the source catalog or strong secondary evidence.

## Visual Evidence Discipline

Use the image mainly for:
- script family signals
- layout complexity
- diagrams, tables, maps, or unusual notation
- marginalia or annotation density
- decoration that meaningfully changes research upside
- signs that the manuscript is more ordinary than the metadata pass suggested

If the thumbnail is too weak to support a claim, say so and lower confidence.

## How Thumbnail Evidence Should Affect Scoring

- If the thumbnail confirms unusual technical or multilingual content, maintain
  or increase score.
- If the thumbnail suggests a standard liturgical or common-text witness,
  decrease score.
- If the thumbnail is inconclusive, keep the score close to the metadata pass
  and say that the image is non-diagnostic.

## Visual Signals That Usually Increase Score

- diagrams, charts, tables, maps
- unusual scripts or mixed scripts
- marginalia or interlinear annotation
- technical notation or symbol systems
- non-standard page architecture
- strong signs of heavy scholarly or practical use

## Visual Signals That Usually Decrease Score

- standard liturgical layout
- generic devotional presentation
- ordinary clean copy of a common text
- decoration that looks conventional rather than content-bearing

## Style

Be concise. Be conservative. Prefer honest uncertainty over impressive fiction.
